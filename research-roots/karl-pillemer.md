  - "Karl Pillemer, a sociologist and gerontologist at Cornell University, led a national survey for *Fault Lines: Fractured Families and How to Mend Them* (2020). It found that roughly a quarter of American adults were estranged from a relative."
  - "He also interviewed more than a hundred people who had reconciled. A common turning point: they stopped needing the other person to agree with their version of the past, focused on the future, adjusted their expectations, and often felt relief even when the relationship stayed imperfect."
concepts:
  - term: Estrangement is common
    def: About one in four adults have cut off a relative.
  - term: Letting go of the shared history
    def: Reconcilers stop requiring agreement about the past.
  - term: Future focus
    def: Rebuilding around what comes next, not who was right.
  - term: Adjusted expectations
    def: A good-enough relationship is still a gain.
context: "Pillemer’s findings are a hopeful counterweight to how stuck family rifts can feel. They also echo the labs’ approach to forgiveness: letting go of the demand that the past be different than it was. We hold this with care. Sometimes distance is the healthy choice, and reconciling is never required."
exercise:
  title: Verdict or future
  time: About 20 minutes, written
  steps:
    - Think of a strained family relationship. Only do this one if contact is safe for you.
    - Write down what you would need them to admit or agree about the past.
    - Now write what you would want the relationship to look like a year from now.
    - Notice which list feels more possible, and which one you’ve been waiting on.
    - If you choose, plan one small, future-focused contact that doesn’t ask them to agree about the past.
  why: Pillemer’s interviews found that people who reconciled usually stopped making agreement about the past a condition. Separating the verdict from the future makes it clearer what you actually want, and whether reconnecting is right for you.
