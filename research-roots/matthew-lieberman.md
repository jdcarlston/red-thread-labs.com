who:
  - Matthew Lieberman, one of the founders of social cognitive neuroscience, led the affect-labeling studies. In [“Putting Feelings Into Words” (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) his team found that naming an emotion lowers activity in the amygdala and engages a regulating area of the prefrontal cortex.
  - That gave a brain basis to what therapists had long observed, and that Dan Siegel calls “name it to tame it.” His book *Social* (2013) argues that our need to connect is as basic as our need for food and shelter.
concepts:
  - term: Affect labeling
    def: Naming a feeling reduces its grip.
  - term: Words as a lever
    def: Language changes emotion, not just describes it.
  - term: The social brain
    def: Connection runs on dedicated brain systems. Social pain is real pain.
  - term: Regulation can be practiced
    def: A skill you build, not a temperament you’re stuck with.
context: "Lieberman is the science under one of the simplest skills we teach: catch the feeling and name it, and you’ve already begun to change it. It’s one of the best-supported small moves in the work, and proof that tiny practices can shift the nervous system."
exercise:
  title: One word for it
  time: Through the day, for a week
  steps:
    - When you notice a strong feeling, pause.
    - "Name it in one or two words, silently or out loud: “frustrated,” “lonely,” “nervous and hopeful.”"
    - If the first word is vague, like “bad,” try a more exact one.
    - Notice whether the intensity shifts after you name it, even slightly.
    - At night, jot down the words you used. Over the week, your emotional vocabulary grows.
  why: Lieberman’s studies show that putting a feeling into words calms the brain’s alarm. More precise words seem to help more, so building a richer emotional vocabulary makes the tool stronger.
