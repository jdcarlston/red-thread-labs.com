tagline: Adult love runs on the same system that kept us close as infants.
who:
  - Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver carried Bowlby and Ainsworth out of the nursery and into adult romance. Their 1987 paper, [“Romantic Love Conceptualized as an Attachment Process,”](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.52.3.511) proposed that the bond between adult partners grows from the same attachment system that bonds an infant to a caregiver.
  - Two questionnaire studies found secure, anxious and avoidant patterns in adults, in roughly the same proportions as in infants, and found that each pattern experiences love differently. The paper launched the field of adult attachment. Shaver and Mario Mikulincer later gathered decades of that research in [*Attachment in Adulthood*](https://www.guilford.com/books/Attachment-in-Adulthood/Mikulincer-Shaver/9781462533817).
concepts:
  - term: Adult attachment styles
    def: Secure, anxious and avoidant patterns carried into romantic love.
  - term: One system, a whole life
    def: Partners become each other’s safe base and haven, the way a caregiver once was.
  - term: Working models in love
    def: Expectations of the self and a partner set how much closeness feels safe, and how conflict goes.
  - term: Continuity, not destiny
    def: Patterns carry forward, but [only moderately](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327957PSPR0602_03). They are habits of relating, not fixed traits.
context: "This is the bridge from childhood to the patterns people bring into a lab: the reason you keep landing in the same kind of relationship is often an attachment style, learned early and running quietly. It makes it reasonable to talk about “your pattern” in adult love. To be honest about the evidence, the link between infancy and adulthood is [real but moderate](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327957PSPR0602_03), which is good news. Paired with the research on [earned security](/lineage/earned-secure-attachment/), it’s why we treat patterns as something you can practice your way out of."
exercise:
  title: Which description sounds like you?
  time: About 10 minutes
  steps:
    - Hazan and Shaver asked people to choose which of three short descriptions best fit how they feel in love. Here they are in our own words:<br>**A.** Getting close comes fairly easily. I’m comfortable relying on a partner and having them rely on me, and I don’t often worry about being left or about someone getting too close.<br>**B.** I’m a bit uneasy getting close. It’s hard to trust a partner completely, and I get tense when someone wants more closeness than I do.<br>**C.** Partners seem less eager to get close than I’d like. I often worry they don’t really love me or won’t stay, and my wish to be very close can scare people off.
    - Pick the one that fits your current or most recent relationship best. Then pick one for the relationship before that.
    - Notice whether the same one shows up twice. Write down one moment this week when it showed up.
    - "Read description A again and ask: in that moment, what is one thing a person who felt that way might have done? Try a small version of it the next time the moment comes."
  why: These three descriptions are the method of the [1987 study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.52.3.511), which found the secure (A), avoidant (B) and anxious (C) patterns in adult love. Later research found these work [more like sliding scales than boxes](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000027), and they [shift over a life](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327957PSPR0602_03). So treat your answer as a starting point for practice, not a label.
