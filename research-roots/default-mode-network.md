who:
  - "In [2001, Marcus Raichle and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.98.2.676) described the default mode network: brain regions most active when the mind turns inward, during rest, daydreaming, memory and thinking about ourselves, and quieter during absorbed outward tasks."
  - Judson Brewer’s team found that [experienced meditators](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1112029108) showed less activity in this network. Robin Carhart-Harris and Karl Friston proposed that the network helps hold our highest-level beliefs and expectations in place, and that [loosening its grip](https://doi.org/10.1124/pr.118.017160) can make those beliefs more open to change.
concepts:
  - term: The default mode
    def: The brain’s inward-turned, self-referencing activity.
  - term: Mind-wandering
    def: Drifting into past, future and stories about ourselves.
  - term: High-level beliefs
    def: Deep expectations about self and world that shape everything below.
  - term: Loosening the grip
    def: Practices like meditation can quiet the network for a time.
context: The labs aim to help people hold their self-story more lightly, in the body and with a witness, without needing any substances to do it. Simple attention practices are one of the best-studied ways to do that.
exercise:
  title: Noting
  time: Ten minutes a day, for a week
  steps:
    - Sit comfortably and rest your attention on your breath.
    - "When your mind wanders, notice where it went and silently label it: “planning,” “remembering,” “worrying,” “judging.”"
    - Then gently return to the breath. No need to scold yourself.
    - Do this for ten minutes.
    - Afterward, note which labels came up most. That’s a glimpse of your default mode.
  why: Brewer’s studies of meditators found lower default mode activity during practices like this one. Labeling where the mind goes, and returning, trains you to notice the self-story instead of being carried by it.
