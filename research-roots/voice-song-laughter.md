tagline: We were bonding with our voices long before we had words.
who:
  - Primates keep their bonds by grooming, one pair at a time. Robin Dunbar’s group at Oxford asked how humans hold together groups far too big to groom. Their answer is the voice. Singing and laughing together can reach a whole room at once.
  - "In [the ice-breaker study](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.150221), Eiluned Pearce, Jacques Launay and Dunbar followed new adult education classes for seven months. Singing classes bonded much faster than craft or creative writing classes. A [later study of a 232-person choir](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2015.10.002) found the same rise in closeness and pain tolerance in the big group as in the small ones."
  - "Laughter does the same work. In [a 2017 brain imaging study](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0688-16.2017), Sandra Manninen, Dunbar and Lauri Nummenmaa found that laughing with close friends releases the brain’s own opioids, the chemistry of grooming and bonding."
  - "Sara Algoe and Laura Kurtz at UNC brought this into couples. They filmed couples telling the story of how they met and counted the laughs. [The more the partners laughed together](https://doi.org/10.1111/pere.12095), the higher they rated their relationship. Laughing alone didn’t count. Laughing together did."
  - "The body joins in too. Björn Vickhoff’s team in Gothenburg found that [when a choir sings in unison](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00334), the singers’ heart rates speed up and slow down together, following the shared breath. Leslie Seltzer found that [a mother’s voice on the phone](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2010.0567) calmed stressed daughters about as well as a hug. Text messages didn’t."
concepts:
  - term: Grooming at a distance
    def: Singing and laughter bond many people at once, the way grooming bonds two.
  - term: The ice-breaker effect
    def: Groups that sing together grow close faster than groups that only talk or make things together.
  - term: Shared laughter
    def: Laughing at the same thing at the same time. A sign you see the world the same way.
  - term: Synchrony
    def: Breath, voice and heartbeat falling into the same rhythm.
  - term: The regulating voice
    def: A warm, familiar voice can calm the body almost as well as touch.
  - term: Laughing at, not with
    def: Laughter during a fight is often defensive or mocking. Only laughter you share bonds you.
framework:
  name: Four ways the voice brings us together
  intro: What the research supports so far, from the strongest evidence to the most personal.
  items:
    - name: Sing together
      def: Group singing builds closeness quickly, even between strangers, and it works in a group of hundreds as well as in a small one.
    - name: Laugh together
      def: Shared laughter releases the brain’s own opioids and tells both people they see the world the same way. In couples it tracks how good the relationship feels.
    - name: Breathe together
      def: Singing in unison, chanting or reciting a prayer slows the breath toward six breaths a minute. Heart rhythms grow stronger and fall into step.
    - name: Speak softly
      def: Hearing a trusted voice lowers stress hormones in a way a written message doesn’t. When someone is struggling, call them.
context: The labs use the voice on purpose. A song or a hum at the start of a session can turn a room of strangers into a group faster than an icebreaker game. Shared laughter is a measure we can watch, not just a nice extra. And in hard moments, a calm voice settles the body before words can. The caution is that laughter can also wound. The labs practice laughing with each other, never at each other.
exercise:
  title: How we met, out loud
  time: About 20 minutes, for a couple, two friends or a small group
  steps:
    - Sit close enough to hear each other’s breath. Take three slow breaths together, out loud on the exhale.
    - Hum one long note together for about a minute. Let your voices find the same pitch without trying too hard.
    - Take turns telling the story of how you met. Each person adds the parts the other leaves out.
    - Notice when you both laugh. Don’t try to make it happen. Just notice.
    - "Afterward, each of you names one moment you laughed together and one thing you heard in the other’s voice."
    - If you’re apart, try it on a call instead of by text.
  why: Humming slows and joins the breath. Telling the how-we-met story is the same task Kurtz and Algoe used to measure shared laughter in couples. It brings back early closeness and makes it easy to laugh together.
