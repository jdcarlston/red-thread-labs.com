tagline: A lasting partnership is built in small moments, on purpose.
who:
  - "John Gottman spent decades watching couples talk and fight in his research lab, measuring heart rate, facial expression and the exact words used. From that data he could [predict which couples would separate](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.2.221) with striking accuracy, and he identified the behaviors that most often came before it: criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling, which he called the Four Horsemen."
  - He and Nan Silver turned the research into *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work*. His studies of newlyweds also found that couples who [turned toward each other’s small bids for attention](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1545-5300.2004.00024.x) were the ones whose relationships lasted.
concepts:
  - term: The Four Horsemen
    def: Criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling. Contempt is the strongest single warning sign.
  - term: Bids and turning toward
    def: Small reaches for attention, and whether we answer them, build or drain trust.
  - term: Accepting influence
    def: Letting your partner’s view change your mind is a mark of lasting couples.
  - term: Perpetual problems
    def: Most conflicts never fully resolve. Lasting couples learn to talk about them kindly.
context: "Gottman gives the labs an evidence-based picture of what healthy partnership looks like day to day. The labs work on what makes those behaviors possible under stress: a settled body, an updated map of what to expect, and practice. His antidote to contempt, a culture of appreciation, is close to the heart of what we mean by being cherished."
exercise:
  title: Catch the bids
  time: A few minutes a day, for a week
  steps:
    - "Choose one close relationship: a partner, a friend, a family member."
    - "For a week, notice their bids: a comment, a question, a touch, a sigh meant to be heard."
    - "Each evening, jot down two or three bids and how you responded: turned toward, turned away, or turned against."
    - Each day, try to turn toward one more bid than you naturally would. A small answer counts.
    - At the end of the week, notice what changed in the relationship’s tone.
  why: Gottman’s newlywed research found that the couples who stayed together turned toward each other’s bids far more often than those who split. Noticing bids is the first skill. Answering them, even in small ways, builds trust over time.
