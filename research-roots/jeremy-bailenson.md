tagline: You can meet through a screen, but some of what two bodies share gets lost.
who:
  - "Jeremy Bailenson founded Stanford’s Virtual Human Interaction Lab, which studies presence, embodiment and communication through technology. His [2021 paper “Nonverbal Overload”](https://doi.org/10.1037/tmb0000030) named four likely causes of video-call fatigue: intense close-up eye contact, constantly seeing yourself, being stuck in place, and the extra effort of reading flattened body language."
  - His book *Experience on Demand* (2018) looks at how strongly the nervous system responds to presence, and how differently it behaves through a frame.
concepts:
  - term: Nonverbal overload
    def: Video calls ask the brain to work harder to read people.
  - term: Self-view
    def: Seeing yourself all day is tiring and self-focusing.
  - term: Presence
    def: The felt sense of really being with someone.
  - term: Mobility
    def: Moving freely helps thinking and connection.
context: Bailenson’s work supports why the labs are in person. Screens are useful, but much of what lets people settle together, timing, touch, eye contact at a natural distance, gets lost or strained through one.
exercise:
  title: Screen versus room
  time: Over a week
  steps:
    - On your next few video calls, hide your self-view and notice how it feels.
    - Try making one call audio-only while you walk.
    - Swap one video call for an in-person meeting or a walk with that person.
    - After each, rate your energy and how connected you felt from 1 to 10.
    - Notice which format leaves you most connected and least drained.
  why: Bailenson’s paper suggests hiding self-view, allowing movement, and taking audio breaks reduce video fatigue. Comparing formats directly shows you what different settings cost and give.
