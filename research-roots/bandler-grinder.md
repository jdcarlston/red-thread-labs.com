who:
  - "Richard Bandler, a mathematics and computer-science student, and John Grinder, a linguist, met at UC Santa Cruz in the early 1970s, in the circle around Gregory Bateson. Their method was modeling: studying not what gifted therapists believed they were doing, but the actual patterns of language and behavior that produced their results. Their main models were Virginia Satir, Fritz Perls and Milton Erickson."
  - "From that came the Meta-Model, in *The Structure of Magic* (1975), and perceptual positions, made explicit in *Frogs into Princes* (1979). To be honest about the evidence: [A 2012 systematic review](https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp12X658287) found little support for NLP as a treatment. Its lasting value, for us, is the modeling method and a handful of precise tools."
concepts:
  - term: Modeling
    def: Studying the exact structure of excellent performance so it can be taught.
  - term: The Meta-Model
    def: "Questions that recover what got left out of what someone said: “What specifically?” “How do you know?”"
  - term: Representational systems
    def: People think in pictures, sounds, feelings and words, in different mixes.
  - term: Perceptual positions
    def: Seeing a situation from your own eyes, from another’s, and from outside both.
context: "Modeling is how the labs are built: we look at what actually works for people who relate well, and turn it into practice. The Meta-Model shows up in how facilitators ask questions, gently recovering the specifics behind words like “always” and “never.”"
exercise:
  title: What, specifically?
  time: About 15 minutes
  steps:
    - Write down three sentences you often say about yourself or your relationships, especially ones with “always,” “never,” “can’t,” “should” or “everyone.”
    - "For each, ask the Meta-Model questions: Always? Every single time? Who, specifically? What would happen if you did? Says who?"
    - Write down any exceptions you find.
    - Rewrite each sentence so it’s more accurate.
    - Notice how the new version feels compared to the old one.
  why: The Meta-Model was built to recover the details that broad generalizations leave out. Testing your own “always” and “never” statements often shows the pattern is less fixed than it sounds, which opens room to change it.
