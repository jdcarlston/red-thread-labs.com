title: Threat Learning

tagline: Threat is learned, and so is safety.
who:
  - "Joseph LeDoux traced the brain circuits of fear learning, centered on the amygdala: a fast pathway that flags danger and fires the body before the thinking brain catches up. His work, summarized in [a 2000 review](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.23.1.155) and in *The Emotional Brain* (1996), explains how an old danger can take over the present in a split second."
  - "It also shows the way out: learned threat responses can weaken through new learning. In later work he separates these automatic threat circuits from the conscious feeling of fear, which he argues is built by the thinking mind."
concepts:
  - term: The amygdala
    def: A rapid threat detector that works faster than conscious thought.
  - term: Fear conditioning
    def: A neutral cue paired with danger becomes a trigger.
  - term: Extinction
    def: New, safe experiences can weaken an old threat response.
  - term: Threat and fear
    def: The body’s alarm and the conscious feeling are different systems.
context: "LeDoux helps explain a line we use in the labs: activation isn’t love. The pull toward someone familiar but wrong for you can be a threat circuit firing, not desire. And his work is hopeful: what was learned can be overlaid with new learning."
exercise:
  title: Old alarm, new evidence
  time: About 15 minutes, then a week of noticing
  steps:
    - "Pick a mild, everyday trigger: a certain tone of voice, a delayed text, a closed door. Avoid your hardest one."
    - Write what your body does when it happens, and what it seems to expect.
    - "Next time it happens, name it: “That’s the old alarm.”"
    - Stay with the situation a little longer than usual and notice what actually happens.
    - Write down the new evidence. Over time, the list becomes a record of safety.
  why: LeDoux’s research shows that threat responses are learned links, and that repeated safe experience can weaken them. Staying present long enough to notice that the feared outcome didn’t happen is how that new learning takes hold.
