tagline: Few motives in human experience are as powerful as the yearning to be understood.
who:
  - Michael P. Nichols is a family therapist and clinical psychologist. After his PhD at the University of Rochester he trained with Salvador Minuchin at the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic and with Murray Bowen in Washington, D.C. He spent seventeen years at Albany Medical College, where he directed family therapy training and outpatient psychiatry, then taught psychology at the College of William & Mary from 1994. He is now professor emeritus there. His textbook *Family Therapy* has trained generations of family therapists, and he wrote books with Minuchin and with [Richard Schwartz](/library/richard-schwartz/), the founder of Internal Family Systems.
  - "His own contribution was to bring the person back into the system. In *The Self in the System* (1987) he argued that family therapy had made “the system” so central that it lost sight of each family member’s own hopes, motives and feelings. In *No Place to Hide* (1991) he wrote about shame, and how much of our energy goes into hiding it or making up for it through defensiveness, arrogance, overwork or keeping people at a distance."
  - "*The Lost Art of Listening* (1995; third edition with Martha B. Straus, 2021) brings all of this into everyday conversation. Most of us think we listen well. Nichols shows how hard it really is: to hear someone, you have to set aside your own need to be heard, the assumptions you came in with, and your reactions, which means giving up control of the conversation for a while. The third edition adds texting, social media, and talking with people across political divides."
concepts:
  - term: The yearning to be understood
    def: Being heard tells us we’re taken seriously and that what we say matters. When the people closest to us don’t listen, it hurts more than almost anything.
  - term: Listening shapes the self
    def: What gets heard and accepted becomes part of who we are. What never gets heard gets pushed out of sight, even from ourselves.
  - term: Suspending your own needs
    def: Listening is active work. Hold back the urge to answer, fix, reassure or tell your own story until the other person has finished.
  - term: Reactivity is contagious
    def: When one person gets defensive, the other usually does too. We react hardest to the things we secretly blame ourselves for.
  - term: We create the listening we get
    def: Speakers shape it too. Say plainly what you want, pick a time they can hear it, and acknowledge their side first.
  - term: Empathy is understanding
    def: Empathy means understanding how something feels from the other person’s side. You can do that and still keep your own view.
context: "Most conversations that go wrong in a lab don’t go wrong because of what someone said. They go wrong when someone stops listening to get ready to answer. Nichols names why that happens and gives both people something to do about it. The listener sets aside their turn and their reactions long enough to understand. The speaker says plainly what they need and picks a time the other person can hear it. He also ties listening to shame. Much of what gets in the way is old hurt. In Practice Labs we treat listening as a skill you practice, not a trait you have or lack. His books draw on decades of clinical work rather than controlled trials. The research that has tested listening directly supports their core claim: people who are listened to well feel more understood, less anxious and clearer about what they think ([Weger and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/10904018.2013.813234); [Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167216675339)). Listening techniques alone don’t guarantee that people will like each other more. What they reliably give is the experience of being understood."
exercise:
  title: Their turn first
  time: About 20 minutes, with a partner or friend
  steps:
    - "Before you start, the speaker says what kind of listening they want: just to be heard, help thinking it through, or advice."
    - The speaker talks for three to five minutes about something that matters to them. The listener’s only job is to understand it. No advice, no stories of your own, no reassuring, no correcting.
    - Notice the urge to take your turn, and let it pass. If you feel defensive, make a mental note to come back to it later, and return to listening.
    - "When they pause, say back what you heard in your own words, including the feeling: “So what’s hardest is…” Then ask, “Did I get that right? Is there more?” Keep going until they say yes, you’ve got it."
    - Switch roles. Afterward, each of you says one thing it was like to be listened to that way, and one reaction you set aside while you were listening.
  why: Nichols identifies the listener’s own needs, assumptions and reactivity as the main things that get in the way, and he puts part of the work on the speaker, who can say what kind of listening they need. Saying back what you heard and checking it is a form of active listening, which helps speakers feel more understood than other kinds of response (Weger and colleagues, 2014). High-quality listening also helps speakers feel less anxious and more aware of their own thinking (Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro, 2017).
