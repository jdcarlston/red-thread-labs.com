who:
  - Michael P. Nichols is a family therapist and psychologist who trained with Murray Bowen and Salvador Minuchin. He spent seventeen years at Albany Medical College, where he directed family therapy training and outpatient psychiatry, then taught psychology at the College of William & Mary from 1994. He is now professor emeritus there. His textbook *Family Therapy: Concepts and Methods* has trained generations of family therapists.
  - "*The Lost Art of Listening* (1995; third edition with Martha B. Straus, 2021) carries that clinical work into everyday life. His argument is that listening is hard not for lack of technique, but because we have to set aside our own need to be heard, our assumptions and our reactions long enough to take someone else in. Being heard, he writes, strengthens our sense of self as well as our connection to each other."
concepts:
  - term: Suspending your own needs
    def: The hardest part of listening is waiting your turn, holding back the urge to answer, fix or tell your own story so the other person can finish.
  - term: Hidden assumptions
    def: What you expect someone to say filters what you actually hear. Listening starts with noticing the story you brought into the conversation.
  - term: Emotional reactivity
    def: When you feel criticized, you defend instead of listen. Staying curious while you’re upset is a skill, and it can be practiced.
  - term: Being heard builds the self
    def: Feeling understood does more than settle a conversation. It helps people know their own minds and feel connected.
context: "Most conversations that go wrong in a lab don’t go wrong because of what someone said. They go wrong when someone stops listening to get ready to answer. Nichols names why that happens: our own need to be heard, the assumptions we bring, and how quickly we get defensive. In Practice Labs we treat listening as a skill you practice, not a trait you have or lack. His book draws on decades of clinical work rather than controlled trials. The research that has tested listening directly supports its core claim: people who are listened to well feel more understood, less anxious and clearer about what they think ([Weger and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/10904018.2013.813234); [Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167216675339)). Listening techniques alone don’t guarantee that people will like each other more. What they reliably give is the experience of being understood."
exercise:
  title: Their turn first
  time: About 15 minutes, with a partner or friend
  steps:
    - Your partner talks for three to five minutes about something that matters to them. Your only job is to understand it. No advice, no stories of your own, no correcting.
    - Notice the urge to take your turn, and let it pass. If you feel a reaction, like the pull to defend or to fix, name it silently and come back to listening.
    - "When they pause, say back what you heard in your own words, including the feeling: “So what’s hardest is…” Then ask, “Did I get that right? Is there more?”"
    - Keep going until they say yes, you’ve got it.
    - Switch roles. Afterward, each of you says one thing it was like to be listened to that way.
  why: Nichols identifies the listener’s own needs, assumptions and reactivity as the main things that get in the way, so the exercise practices setting each of them aside. Saying back what you heard and checking it is a form of active listening, which helps speakers feel more understood than other kinds of response (Weger and colleagues, 2014). High-quality listening also helps speakers feel less anxious and more aware of their own thinking (Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro, 2017).
