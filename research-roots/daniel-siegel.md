who:
  - "Dan Siegel, a psychiatrist, founded interpersonal neurobiology in *The Developing Mind* (1999). It brings neuroscience, attachment and mindfulness into one frame: the mind is embodied and relational, and health looks like *integration*, distinct parts of a brain, a person or a relationship that stay linked."
  - He coined “mindsight” for the ability to perceive your own inner world and other people’s, popularized “name it to tame it,” and described the “window of tolerance,” the range in which we can feel strongly and still think.
concepts:
  - term: Integration
    def: Health as different parts working together, between rigidity and chaos.
  - term: Mindsight
    def: The skill of seeing the inner world, your own and others’.
  - term: Window of tolerance
    def: The zone where you can feel a lot and still respond, not react.
  - term: The relational brain
    def: Attuned relationships shape how the brain regulates.
context: "Siegel’s idea of integration, distinct and then linked, describes what we hope people leave with: a clearer sense of their own self, and the ability to join another person without losing it. His window of tolerance is the working zone of every lab. We practice at the edge of it, never far outside."
exercise:
  title: Your window of tolerance
  time: About 20 minutes
  steps:
    - Draw a wide band across a page. Label the top edge “too much” (racing, angry, panicky) and the bottom edge “too little” (numb, foggy, shut down).
    - Inside the band, write what you’re like when you’re in your window.
    - Above and below, write your personal signs of leaving it.
    - List what pushes you out, and what brings you back in.
    - "This week, when you notice you’ve left your window, name it out loud or on paper: “I’m above my window.”"
  why: Siegel’s window of tolerance gives people a simple picture of regulation. Mapping your own signs makes it easier to catch yourself early, and naming the state is itself a regulating act.
