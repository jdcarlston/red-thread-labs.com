tagline: Perspectives aren’t cameras you pick up. They’re capacities you grow.
who:
  - "NLP describes three perceptual positions: your own view, another person’s, and an observer’s view of both. In NLP they were first treated as options you could pick at any moment. Developmental research suggests something deeper. Robert Selman’s studies in *The Growth of Interpersonal Understanding* (1980) showed that children move through stages of perspective-taking as they grow, and adults keep developing too."
  - Modern experiments back up the power of these moves. [Taking another person’s perspective](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.4.708) reduced stereotyping in one well-known study, and [stepping back to an observer’s view](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01600.x) let people think about painful memories with less distress.
concepts:
  - term: First position
    def: Your own eyes, feelings and wants. It needs a real self to stand in.
  - term: Second position
    def: Genuinely stepping into another person’s experience, and finding your way back.
  - term: Observer position
    def: Seeing both people at once from a calm, caring distance.
  - term: Capacity, not trick
    def: Each position deepens with practice and maturity.
context: "The idea that these positions are capacities that grow, not just techniques, came to JD through Dr. McDonald’s teaching, and she built the nine-rotation curriculum around developing them. It’s why the labs are a series: you can’t fully stand in someone else’s shoes until you have solid ground of your own to return to."
exercise:
  title: The fly on the wall
  time: About 15 minutes
  steps:
    - Recall a recent upsetting conversation. Choose a medium one.
    - First, replay it through your own eyes for a moment. Notice what you feel.
    - Now step back and picture the scene from across the room, as a kind observer. See both people.
    - "Talk to yourself from there, using your own name or “you”: “JD, what’s going on for you here? What do you need?”"
    - "From there, ask: what was each person feeling and needing?"
    - Write down anything you see from the observer position that you couldn’t see from inside.
  why: Kross and Ayduk’s experiments found that reflecting on a painful memory from a distanced, observer view reduced distress and helped people make sense of it. Kross and colleagues later found that talking to yourself by name, or as “you,” has the same steadying effect before a hard moment. Practicing both builds the observer position as a capacity you can call on.
