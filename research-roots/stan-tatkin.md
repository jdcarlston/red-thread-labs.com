tagline: Two people can agree to be each other’s safe place.
who:
  - Stan Tatkin is a couple therapist who developed PACT, the Psychobiological Approach to Couple Therapy. It draws on attachment theory, developmental neuroscience and the regulation of arousal, and treats a couple as two nervous systems that can learn to look after each other.
  - "In *Wired for Love* (2012) he describes the “couple bubble,” a shared agreement to protect each other’s safety, and *secure functioning*: a relationship run on fairness, justice and sensitivity, where neither person’s needs come at the other’s expense."
concepts:
  - term: The couple bubble
    def: A shared commitment to protect each other’s safety, inside and outside the relationship.
  - term: Secure functioning
    def: The relationship comes first, and both people are cared for.
  - term: Anchors, islands and waves
    def: His everyday words for secure, more distancing, and more anxious styles of attachment.
  - term: Co-regulation
    def: Partners acting as each other’s first line of calm.
context: "Tatkin takes attachment and arousal regulation into everyday couple practice. In the labs that shows up as a simple idea: two people walking into a hard situation, a family holiday or a crowded party, can agree ahead of time to be each other’s anchor. Security becomes something you set up together, not something you hope for."
exercise:
  title: A reunion ritual
  time: One minute a day, for a week
  steps:
    - "Pick one person you live with or see often: a partner, a child, a close friend."
    - Each time you see each other after time apart, stop what you’re doing for about a minute.
    - Turn toward them, make eye contact, and greet them as if you’re glad they’re here, because you are.
    - Notice how your body and theirs settle, or don’t, over the week.
    - At the end of the week, talk about whether you want to keep it.
  why: Tatkin recommends small, repeated rituals of greeting and reunion because the first moments after time apart set the tone for what follows. A minute of full attention tells both nervous systems “you’re safe with me” before the day’s stress gets a vote.
