tagline: Vulnerability is where connection starts, and trust is how it grows.
who:
  - Brené Brown, a research professor of social work at the University of Houston, studied shame, vulnerability and what she calls wholeheartedness through thousands of interviews. Her [shame resilience theory](https://doi.org/10.1606/1044-3894.3483) describes how people recognize shame and move through it with empathy.
  - "She popularized the distinction between shame (“I am bad”) and guilt (“I did something bad”), building on June Tangney’s experimental work, and found that people with a strong sense of connection shared the courage to be imperfect and seen. Her findings are qualitative: research-informed language for what people feel, more than a tested method of change."
  - "Her later work turns to courage and trust. In *Dare to Lead* (2018) she describes courage as four skills anyone can learn: rumbling with vulnerability, living into your values, braving trust, and learning to rise after a fall. She borrows Charles Feltman’s definition of trust, choosing to risk making something you value vulnerable to someone else’s actions, and breaks it into seven elements she calls BRAVING."
concepts:
  - term: Shame resilience
    def: Shame grows in secrecy and judgment and shrinks when spoken and met with empathy.
  - term: Vulnerability
    def: The courage to be seen without guarantees.
  - term: Empathy and sympathy
    def: Feeling with someone, not looking down from outside.
  - term: “The story I’m telling myself”
    def: Naming the quick first-draft story so it can be checked.
  - term: Belonging and fitting in
    def: Fitting in changes you to be accepted. Belonging asks you to be who you are.
  - term: Courage is a skill
    def: Bravery isn’t a trait you have or lack. It can be practiced, one hard conversation at a time.
  - term: The marble jar
    def: Trust is built in small moments, like marbles added to a jar, and lost the same way.
framework:
  name: BRAVING, the seven elements of trust
  intro: Brown breaks trust into seven parts so you can see which one is strong and which one needs repair. It works with someone else, and it works turned inward, because trusting yourself runs on the same seven.
  items:
    - name: Boundaries
      mark: B
      def: You respect my limits, and you ask when you’re not sure what’s okay. You can say no, and so can I.
    - name: Reliability
      mark: R
      def: You do what you say you’ll do, over and over, and you’re honest about what you can take on.
    - name: Accountability
      mark: A
      def: When you get it wrong, you own it, apologize and make it right.
    - name: Vault
      mark: V
      def: What I tell you in confidence stays with you, and I see you keep other people’s confidences too.
    - name: Integrity
      mark: I
      def: You choose courage over comfort, and you live your values instead of only talking about them.
    - name: Nonjudgment
      mark: N
      def: We can both ask for what we need and say how we feel without being judged for it.
    - name: Generosity
      mark: G
      def: You assume the best about my intentions, words and actions, and check with me before deciding otherwise.
context: "Brown names the fear at the center of many patterns: that if people really saw us, they’d leave. Her work on shame, vulnerability, trust and courage runs through much of what we do. The labs give people safe practice at being seen, so it gets more survivable each time. Trust is how two people learn it’s safe to be seen, a marble at a time, and courage is what it takes to go first. BRAVING gives people words for which part of trust is strong between them and which part needs repair."
exercise:
  title: The story I’m telling myself
  time: About 10 minutes
  steps:
    - "Think of a recent moment that stung: a short reply, being left out, a look."
    - "Finish this sentence honestly, even if it sounds dramatic: “The story I’m telling myself is…”"
    - List what you actually know, and what you’re guessing.
    - Write one other possible story.
    - "If it feels safe, try saying it to the person: “The story I’m telling myself is… Can you help me understand?”"
  why: Brown uses this phrase to catch the fast stories our minds write under stress. Saying it out loud owns the story as yours and invites the other person to share theirs, instead of acting on a guess.
