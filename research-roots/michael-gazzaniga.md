tagline: The story you tell about why is not always the reason.
who:
  - "Michael Gazzaniga, one of the founders of cognitive neuroscience, ran landmark split-brain studies with Roger Sperry in people whose brain hemispheres had been surgically separated. He found what he called the interpreter: a left-hemisphere system that constantly explains our behavior and builds the sense of one continuous “I.”"
  - When the right hemisphere acted on information the left couldn’t see, the interpreter made up a plausible reason on the spot and believed it. His [2005 review](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn1723) and books such as *Who’s in Charge?* (2011) describe how much of our experienced self is this ongoing narration.
concepts:
  - term: The interpreter
    def: A brain system that explains what we do, even when it has to guess.
  - term: Confabulation
    def: Confidently giving a reason that isn’t the real one.
  - term: The narrative self
    def: Much of who we think we are is a story the brain keeps telling.
  - term: Story is not cause
    def: The explanation and the actual cause can be different things.
context: Gazzaniga helps the labs explain why insight alone often doesn’t change a pattern. The reasons we give ourselves are partly stories. Noticing that frees people to try new behavior and watch what happens, instead of arguing with the story.
exercise:
  title: Three other reasons
  time: About 10 minutes
  steps:
    - Think of something you did recently that surprised you, or that you regret.
    - Write the reason you gave yourself for doing it.
    - Now write three other possible reasons, including ones about your body (tired, hungry), your setting, or old habits.
    - Notice whether any of them feel as true, or truer.
    - Hold your first reason a little more loosely next time.
  why: Gazzaniga’s research shows the brain readily invents explanations for behavior. Generating alternatives is a simple way to remember that your first explanation is one story among several.
