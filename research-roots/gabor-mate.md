tagline: Naming the wound is where it starts. Changing the pattern is the work.
who:
  - "Gabor Maté is a Hungarian-Canadian physician who worked in family, palliative and addiction medicine. Through *When the Body Says No* (2003), *In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts* (2008) and *The Myth of Normal* (2022, with Daniel Maté), he became one of the best-known public voices on trauma. His most-quoted line: trauma is not what happens to you, it’s what happens inside you as a result."
  - His approach is called Compassionate Inquiry. Some of his broadest claims, especially about trauma as a cause of illness and addiction, go further than the research supports and are debated.
concepts:
  - term: Trauma as inner response
    def: The wound is what happens inside, not only the event.
  - term: Authenticity and attachment
    def: A child may trade being real for keeping the bond, and carry that trade into adult life.
  - term: Not why the addiction, but why the pain
    def: Addiction read as an attempt to escape suffering.
  - term: The myth of normal
    def: Much of what we call normal is shaped by stress nobody names.
context: "Maté is here as a contrast as much as a source. He is very good at helping people name what happened. The labs are more interested in what happens next: practicing new responses until the old pattern loosens. We keep his plain language for the trade between authenticity and attachment, because it describes so many people’s first strategy for being loved."
exercise:
  title: The trade you made
  time: About 20 minutes, written
  steps:
    - "Finish this sentence a few different ways: “To stay close to the people I needed, I learned to…”"
    - Pick the one that still runs today. Where does it show up?
    - Write what it protects you from, and what it costs you now.
    - "Choose one small, low-risk moment this week to do the opposite: say a true preference, decline something, ask for help."
    - Afterward, note what actually happened, compared to what you feared.
  why: "Maté’s idea that children trade authenticity for attachment fits what attachment research shows: kids adapt to keep their caregivers close. Testing the old rule with small real experiences, rather than only understanding it, is what gives the nervous system new evidence."
