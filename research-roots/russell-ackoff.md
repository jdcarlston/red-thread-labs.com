tagline: A system is the product of how its parts interact.
who:
  - Russell Ackoff (1919–2009) trained as an architect, became a pioneer of operations research, and spent most of his career at the Wharton School teaching organizations to think in systems. In 1979 he [broke with his own field](https://doi.org/10.1057/jors.1979.22), arguing that it had narrowed into math for isolated problems while real managers face whole systems of problems that change together. He called those *messes*.
  - "His best-known lines are about wholes: improve each part of a system separately and you can make the whole worse, because what matters is how the parts fit. He also gave us the data, information, knowledge, understanding and wisdom hierarchy (the DIKW pyramid), first given as a talk in 1988."
concepts:
  - term: Messes
    def: Problems that tangle together, so fixing one alone rarely holds.
  - term: Dissolving a problem
    def: Changing the system so the problem can’t come up again.
  - term: Idealized design
    def: Design what you’d build today if you were starting fresh, then work back from there.
  - term: Data to wisdom
    def: Data, information, knowledge, understanding, wisdom. Each level answers a deeper question.
context: "Ackoff is a big part of JD’s engineering leadership practice, and his DIKW pyramid shapes how Red Thread Labs thinks about learning. In relationships, most of what hurts is a mess, not a single problem. The labs work the way he taught: look at how the pieces interact, then change the design."
exercise:
  title: Dissolve it
  time: About 20 minutes
  steps:
    - Pick a problem that keeps coming back in a relationship or a team.
    - Write down everyone involved and what each person does when it happens.
    - Ask what you’d design if you were setting up this relationship or team from scratch today.
    - Compare that design with how things work now. Look for the place where the problem gets made.
    - Choose one change to the design so the problem has nowhere to start.
  why: Ackoff described four ways to handle a problem. You can ignore it, settle for good enough, find the best answer, or redesign the system so the problem goes away. The last one, dissolving, lasts longest because it changes how the parts interact.
