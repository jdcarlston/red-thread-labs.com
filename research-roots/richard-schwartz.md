tagline: There are no bad parts.
who:
  - "Richard Schwartz trained as a family-systems therapist and kept hearing clients describe distinct inner “parts,” each with its own feelings and agenda. He mapped them as an inner family: protective managers, reactive firefighters, and the young, hurt exiles they guard, all around a core Self that is calm, curious and compassionate."
  - Internal Family Systems (IFS) treats change as stepping back from a part, hearing what it protects, and letting the Self lead. It stands in a long line of parts work, from Jung to Voice Dialogue. Research on IFS is growing but still early; [a 2013 trial](https://doi.org/10.3899/jrheum.121465) in people with rheumatoid arthritis was one of the first.
concepts:
  - term: The Self
    def: A calm, curious, compassionate core that is always there underneath.
  - term: Protectors and exiles
    def: Parts that guard against pain, and the hurt parts they protect.
  - term: No bad parts
    def: Every part is trying to help, even the destructive ones.
  - term: Unblending
    def: Stepping back from a part so you can see it, rather than being run by it.
context: Schwartz gives the labs a kind way to work with the parts of us that sabotage closeness. The protector that pushes people away is trying to keep something young and hurt safe. You don’t fight it. You get curious about it.
exercise:
  title: Meet a protector
  time: About 15 minutes
  steps:
    - "Think of a reaction you don’t like in yourself: snapping, shutting down, people-pleasing, scrolling."
    - Picture that reaction as a part of you. Where do you feel it in your body? What does it look or sound like?
    - Notice how you feel toward it. If you feel angry or ashamed at it, ask those feelings to step back a little, until you feel curious.
    - "Ask the part: “What are you afraid would happen if you didn’t do this?” Listen for the answer."
    - Thank it for trying to protect you. Write down what you learned.
  why: This follows the basic IFS steps of finding a part, unblending, and asking about its fear. Approaching a reaction with curiosity instead of judgment often softens it, and shows what it’s been protecting.
