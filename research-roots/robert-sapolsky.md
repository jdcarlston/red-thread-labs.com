tagline: A stress response built for lions is running your love life.
who:
  - Robert Sapolsky, a Stanford neuroendocrinologist, explained how the stress response, built for a short sprint away from a predator, wears down the body and brain when it stays switched on. *Why Zebras Don’t Get Ulcers* made stress physiology clear to a wide audience.
  - His decades studying baboons in Kenya showed how [social rank and social stress affect health](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1106477). People, he points out, can trigger the full lion response with nothing but a thought.
  - "A zebra’s stress response does exactly what it was built for. It switches on to escape the lion, and once the danger has passed, it switches off and the zebra goes back to grazing. Ours often never switches off, because the thing we’re running from is a worry, a relationship or a bill. Sapolsky also names what makes the same stressor feel worse: having no outlet for the frustration, no support, no way to predict it, no control over it, and a sense that things are getting worse. Emily and Amelia Nagoski build their idea of [completing the stress cycle](/library/emily-nagoski/) on this same physiology."
concepts:
  - term: The stress response
    def: A cascade of hormones, including cortisol, that readies the body to act.
  - term: Short versus long
    def: Built for a sprint. Costly when it never switches off.
  - term: The cost of cortisol
    def: Chronic stress can affect memory, learning and openness.
  - term: Thought as threat
    def: Humans can set off the alarm by worrying about the future.
  - term: What makes stress worse
    def: No outlet for frustration, no support, no predictability, no control, and a sense that things are getting worse.
framework:
  name: The stress cycle, start to finish
  intro: The stress response in the order the body runs it. The trouble starts when the last step never comes.
  items:
    - name: A stressor
      def: Anything that knocks the body out of balance, from a lion to a thought about Monday.
    - name: The fast alarm
      def: Within seconds, the nervous system releases adrenaline. Heart rate, breathing and blood pressure go up.
    - name: The slower wave
      def: Over the next minutes, the brain signals the adrenal glands to release cortisol, which keeps the response going.
    - name: Everything else waits
      def: Energy goes to the muscles. Digestion, growth, reproduction and repair are put on hold.
    - name: The body acts
      def: It runs, fights or hides, using what it got ready.
    - name: The switch-off
      def: Cortisol tells the brain to shut the response down, and the body returns to rest and repair. The Nagoskis call finishing this step completing the cycle.
context: Sapolsky explains why a chronically braced body struggles to bond or learn, and why regulation is a precondition for change, not a luxury. The labs settle the system first because it’s hard to practice something new while the alarm is ringing.
exercise:
  title: Is there a lion?
  time: About 15 minutes
  steps:
    - List the three things that have stressed you most this week.
    - "For each, ask: is this happening right now, or am I bracing for something that might happen?"
    - For anything happening now, write one small action you can take.
    - For anything you’re bracing for, write what you’d do if it did happen, then set it down.
    - "Choose one daily way to switch the stress response off: a walk, time with a friend, a few slow out-breaths."
    - "For the stress that won’t go away, check Sapolsky’s list: where could you add an outlet, some support, a little predictability or a bit of control?"
  why: Sapolsky shows the stress response is useful for real, present threats and harmful when it runs on anticipation. Separating the two helps you act where you can and recover where you can’t, which is what the body was built for.
