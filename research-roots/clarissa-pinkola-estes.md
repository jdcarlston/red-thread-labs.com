who:
  - Clarissa Pinkola Estés is a Jungian analyst, poet and *cantadora*, a keeper of old stories, raised in Latina and Hungarian storytelling traditions. *Women Who Run With the Wolves* (1992) spent years on bestseller lists.
  - "In it she uses fairy tales and myths as maps of the psyche: how a person’s wild, instinctive nature gets tamed or buried, and how it can be recovered. She treats stories not as entertainment but as medicine."
concepts:
  - term: The wild nature
    def: An instinctive, creative core that can be buried but not lost.
  - term: Story as medicine
    def: Old tales hold instructions for living.
  - term: Taming
    def: The ways a person learns to be smaller than they are.
  - term: Recovery
    def: Finding the way back, often through descent and return.
context: "Estés connects to the poetic side of the labs and to JD’s writing as Roz Madder: story as a way of feeling what can’t be explained. Sometimes a myth reaches what a model can’t."
exercise:
  title: Your own tale
  time: About 30 minutes
  steps:
    - Think of a time you learned to be smaller, quieter or more agreeable than you were.
    - "Write it as a short fairy tale, starting “Once there was…” Use images: a forest, a cage, a voice, an animal."
    - Let the story include a moment when the character remembers who they are.
    - Don’t worry about quality. Let it be strange.
    - Read it aloud and notice what it tells you that a plain description wouldn’t.
  why: Estés uses story to reach parts of the psyche that analysis doesn’t. Telling your own experience as a tale gives distance and image, which often lets new meaning in.
