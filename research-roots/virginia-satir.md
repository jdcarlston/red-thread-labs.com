title: Survival Stances
people: Virginia Satir (1916–1988)
field: Family therapy
tagline: The stance is not the person. The survival strategy is not the self.
who:
  - Virginia Satir, often called the mother of family therapy, mapped how people protect themselves under relational stress. In *Conjoint Family Therapy* (1964) and *Peoplemaking* (1972) she described four survival stances, blaming, placating, being super-reasonable and being irrelevant, and one congruent stance, leveling.
  - "She insisted these stances are intelligent: adaptations that once made sense and then got stuck. Bandler and Grinder modeled her work directly when developing NLP."
concepts:
  - term: Blaming
    def: Attacking to feel strong.
  - term: Placating
    def: Agreeing to stop the threat, at the cost of yourself.
  - term: Super-reasonable
    def: Going cold and analytical to avoid feeling.
  - term: Irrelevant
    def: Changing the subject or deflecting when it’s too much.
  - term: Leveling
    def: Open, honest and present. The only stance where real contact happens.
context: Satir’s stances are a cornerstone of how the labs work with patterns under stress. The stance that drives people away is usually trying to keep them close. Seeing it that way makes it possible to choose differently without shame.
exercise:
  title: Catch your stance
  time: About 15 minutes, then a week of noticing
  steps:
    - Read the four survival stances. Which do you fall into most under stress? Most people have a favorite.
    - Recall a recent moment you used it. What were you protecting?
    - "Write what a leveling response would have sounded like: what you honestly felt and wanted, said plainly and kindly."
    - This week, when you notice your stance starting, take a breath and try one leveling sentence.
    - Note what happens.
  why: Satir taught that people can move from survival stances to leveling once they recognize the stance and what it protects. Practicing one honest sentence at the moment of stress is how that shift starts.
