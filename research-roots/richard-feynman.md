title: Sum Over Paths
people: Richard Feynman (1918–1988)

tagline: Everything that could happen contributes.
who:
  - Richard Feynman helped found quantum electrodynamics and invented the [path-integral approach](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.20.367) to quantum mechanics. In it, a particle is treated as taking every possible path at once, each contributing to the outcome, and what we observe emerges from how those paths combine and cancel.
  - "His book *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter* (1985) explained it to a general audience. We use the idea here only as a way of thinking about choices, not as a claim that the mind works like a particle."
concepts:
  - term: Sum over histories
    def: Every possible path contributes to what actually happens.
  - term: Interference
    def: Paths can reinforce or cancel each other.
  - term: Don’t fool yourself
    def: "His first principle of science, from his 1974 Caltech talk “Cargo Cult Science”: “you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool.”"
  - term: A way of thinking
    def: Here, a metaphor for how many small influences shape a result.
context: "As a metaphor, Feynman’s sum over paths is a reminder that any outcome is shaped by many small, unseen influences. His scientific ethic matters to us even more directly: in the labs you are the scientist of your own life, and the first discipline of a scientist is to look honestly for the evidence that would prove you wrong."
exercise:
  title: Try to prove yourself wrong
  time: About 15 minutes, then a week of watching
  steps:
    - Write down one belief about a relationship that you feel sure of, like “She never listens to me” or “If I speak up, he’ll pull away.”
    - "Write what you would expect to see this week if the belief is true, and what you would see if it’s false. Be specific."
    - For one week, look as hard for the evidence that it’s false as for the evidence that it’s true. Write both down as they happen.
    - At the end of the week, count both columns honestly.
    - Rewrite the belief so it fits everything you saw, even if that makes it less tidy.
  why: Feynman held that honest science means bending over backwards to report what could prove you wrong, because the person easiest to fool is yourself. Beliefs about the people we love work like untested hypotheses. Looking for disconfirming evidence is how you find out which ones hold up.
