tagline: A feeling you can describe on ten scales is a feeling you can stay with.
who:
  - "The Sphere of Emotions is JD Carlston’s instrument for reading a feeling closely enough to work with it. It’s the companion to the [Sphere of Somata](/library/sphere-of-somata/). The Sphere of Somata maps the sensations in the body. The Sphere of Emotions reads what those sensations add up to, because an emotion, in this work, is somata with a story attached."
  - "It has ten somas, the dimensions any feeling can be read on. Each soma comes with the question that finds it, and its somata are the readings along its scale, seventy-five in all. It’s built from the modality characteristics of the Destination Method. The move isn’t to name the feeling and file it away. It’s to describe it precisely enough that you can stay with it, and then choose."
concepts:
  - term: Soma and somata
    def: A soma is one dimension of a feeling, like intensity or location. Its somata are the readings along that scale.
  - term: Emotion is weather
    def: A spread of somas moving together, not a verdict about you. It changes, and it can be described.
  - term: Locate, don’t fix
    def: Place the feeling on each scale without trying to change it yet. Locating comes first.
  - term: Naming is the beginning
    def: Naming a feeling isn’t the end of the work. It’s what makes choosing possible.
framework:
  name: The ten somas of any feeling
  intro: Start with whichever soma is loudest, ask its question out loud, and place the feeling on its scale. Then move around the ring until the whole weather is described.
  items:
    - name: Quality
      def: Is it pleasant, unpleasant or neutral?
    - name: Arousal
      def: How activated or calm is your system right now?
    - name: Location
      def: Where in your body do you feel it most? Point to it.
    - name: Label
      def: If it had a name, what would it be? It can have more than one.
    - name: Intensity
      def: How strong is it, from a trace to overwhelming?
    - name: Complexity
      def: Is it one clear feeling, two in tension, or many at once?
    - name: Duration
      def: How long has it been here, from just now to as long as you can remember?
    - name: Familiarity
      def: Is it old and familiar, or new territory?
    - name: Direction
      def: Does it seem to come from inside you, from outside, or both?
    - name: Permissibility
      def: How much permission do you give yourself to feel it fully?
context: "People often come into a lab with one word for a lot of feeling: “bad,” “stressed,” “fine.” The Sphere of Emotions slows that down. Ten simple questions turn a blur into something you can see, stay with and work with, and the Permissibility scale often shows where the real work is. Research backs the core of it. Feelings are built from body signals and the meaning we give them ([Barrett, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsw154)). They map onto pleasantness and activation ([Russell, 1980](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0077714)) and onto specific places in the body ([Nummenmaa and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1321664111)). Putting feelings into words calms the brain’s alarm response ([Lieberman and colleagues, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x)), and finer distinctions between feelings help people regulate them ([Kashdan, Barrett & McKnight, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721414550708)). The instrument itself comes from practice and hasn’t been tested in a trial."
exercise:
  title: Read the weather
  time: About 15 minutes
  steps:
    - Choose a feeling that’s here now, or one that keeps coming back.
    - Find the soma that’s loudest, maybe Location or Intensity, and ask its question out loud.
    - Place the feeling on that scale. Don’t try to change it, just locate it.
    - Move around the other nine somas the same way, one question at a time.
    - Read back the whole weather in a few sentences, and notice what you can choose now that you couldn’t before.
  why: Describing a feeling on several scales is a form of emotion differentiation, which research links to better regulation. Saying it in words also eases the brain’s alarm response. Locating without fixing keeps you with the feeling long enough to learn what it’s telling you.
