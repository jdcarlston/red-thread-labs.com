# On the Research Radar it sits with the "We" pages: it happens between two people.
quadrant: we
title: Kything
people: JD Carlston · original practice, inspired by Madeleine L’Engle (1918–2007)
field: Original practice · Fantasy literature
tagline: Spoken communion through every sense.
who:
  - "Madeleine L’Engle was an American writer best known for *A Wrinkle in Time* (1962). In its sequel, *A Wind in the Door* (1973), she introduced “kything”: her characters share thoughts and feelings without words, so fully that one can almost see through the other’s eyes and feel through their senses."
  - She found the word in an old Scots dictionary, where to kythe means to make known, to show, to let yourself be seen. It comes from the same Old English root as “kith,” the people you know well, as in “kith and kin.” Where Heinlein’s “grok” names understanding something so completely it becomes part of you, kything names making yourself known to someone else, and letting them all the way in.
concepts:
  - term: Kything
    def: A verbal sensory communion. You share an experience so fully, through every sense, that the person listening can almost live it with you.
  - term: Every sense
    def: What you saw, heard, smelled and tasted, what touched your skin, and what moved inside your body.
  - term: Inside and outside
    def: Exteroception is what comes in from the world around you. Interoception is what you notice inside your body. Kything uses both.
  - term: Spoken and silent
    def: Kything can include silence, and silence is powerful in its own way. Putting every sense into words lets the listener witness far more of what you lived.
  - term: Naming
    def: In her story, Meg’s task is to Name someone, to know them so fully that they become more truly themselves.
  - term: A listener who has said yes
    def: The listener agrees to give their full attention to this one experience for a set amount of time.
context: "Kything as a lab practice is JD Carlston’s original work, inspired by L’Engle’s books. It’s a verbal sensory communion, a deeper level of sharing. One person describes an experience in as much detail as they can, through every sense: what they saw, heard, smelled and tasted, what touched their skin, and what was happening inside their body. The other person has agreed to listen, for a set amount of time, to just that experience. The detail is what lets the listener step inside it. It draws on the sense-based language of [NLP](/library/bandler-grinder/) and the [Destination Method](/library/robert-dee-mcdonald/), and on [interoception](/library/bud-craig/), our sense of what’s happening inside the body. L’Engle’s telepathy is fiction. What the research does support is that being listened to with full attention helps people feel understood and see their own experience more clearly ([Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167216675339))."
exercise:
  title: One moment, every sense
  time: About 20 minutes, with a partner
  steps:
    - "Agree on the time first: five minutes for the speaker. The listener agrees to stay with this one experience, with no questions until the end."
    - Pick one moment when you were paying close attention to everything around you, like a meal, a walk, or a few seconds with someone you love. A short moment is enough.
    - Describe everything. What you saw, the colors, light and shapes. What you heard, smelled and tasted. What touched your skin. What was happening inside your body, like your breath, your heartbeat, warmth or tightness. When you think you’re done, find one more detail.
    - "Listener: take it in as if you were there. When the time is up, describe what you experienced while you listened: what you could see, hear and feel."
    - Switch roles. Afterward, talk about what it was like to be let in that far, and to let someone in.
  why: Describing a moment through every sense slows you down and brings you back into the moment itself, not the story you usually tell about it. NLP calls these the representational systems, because we remember and imagine in pictures, sounds, feelings, smells and tastes. Adding what you felt inside your body brings in interoception. A listener who has agreed to stay with one experience for a set time can take it in fully, which is as close as we can get to L’Engle’s kything. You can also kythe in silence, sitting together without words. That has its own power, but the listener can witness much less of what you lived, so in the labs we lead with the spoken version.
