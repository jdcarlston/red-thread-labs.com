who:
  - Ken Wilber set out to integrate psychology, philosophy and contemplative traditions without reducing any one to another. Across more than twenty books, including *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality* (1995) and *Integral Psychology* (2000), he developed Integral Theory and its four-quadrant map.
  - "Two of his distinctions are especially useful: states versus stages, the difference between a temporary experience and a lasting change, and translation versus transformation, making your current way of being more comfortable versus actually changing it. His work is a philosophical synthesis, not empirical research."
concepts:
  - term: Four quadrants
    def: The inner individual (I), outer individual (it), inner collective (we), and outer collective (its).
  - term: States and stages
    def: A peak experience is a state. A lasting shift is a stage.
  - term: Transcend and include
    def: Each stage includes and goes beyond the one before.
  - term: Translation and transformation
    def: Getting more comfortable versus actually changing.
context: Wilber’s states-and-stages idea explains why the labs are a series. One powerful evening is a state. Coming back, practicing, and testing it in your life is how a state becomes a stage.
exercise:
  title: Four views of one problem
  time: About 20 minutes
  steps:
    - Pick a challenge in your life, like loneliness or a recurring fight.
    - "I: What’s happening inside you? Thoughts, feelings, beliefs."
    - "It: What can be observed? Behavior, body, sleep, health."
    - "We: What shared meanings or culture surround it? Family, friends, community norms."
    - "Its: What systems or structures shape it? Work schedules, money, housing, technology. Then pick one step in the quadrant you’ve been ignoring."
  why: Wilber’s quadrants are a way to avoid “nothing but” explanations. Looking at a problem from all four often reveals a lever you’ve been missing.
