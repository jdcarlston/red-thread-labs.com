title: Self-Compassion
people: Kristin Neff (b. 1966)
field: Psychology
tagline: Treat yourself as you would a good friend.
who:
  - "Kristin Neff, a psychologist at the University of Texas at Austin, [defined self-compassion](https://doi.org/10.1080/15298860309032) as three things: self-kindness instead of self-judgment, common humanity instead of isolation, and mindfulness instead of being swallowed by the feeling. She built the [Self-Compassion Scale](https://doi.org/10.1080/15298860309027) to measure it."
  - With Christopher Germer she created the [Mindful Self-Compassion program](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.21923). Her research links self-compassion to resilience and lower anxiety and depression, without the comparison and fragility that can come with self-esteem.
concepts:
  - term: Self-kindness
    def: Warmth instead of attack when you struggle or fail.
  - term: Common humanity
    def: Imperfection is shared. It’s not proof you’re alone.
  - term: Mindfulness
    def: Holding pain in balanced awareness, neither ignoring nor drowning in it.
  - term: Fierce self-compassion
    def: "The protective side: setting limits and standing up for yourself."
context: Neff’s work underlies how the labs handle mistakes and repair. A kind inner voice makes it easier to admit what went wrong and come back, where a harsh one tends to make people hide.
exercise:
  title: A self-compassion break
  time: About 3 minutes, whenever you need it
  steps:
    - When you notice you’re struggling, pause and put a hand somewhere comforting, like your chest.
    - "Say to yourself: “This is a moment of suffering.” (Or “This is hard.”) That’s mindfulness."
    - "Say: “Struggling is part of being human. I’m not alone in this.” That’s common humanity."
    - "Say: “May I be kind to myself.” Or ask: “What would I say to a friend right now?” and say that to yourself."
    - Notice any shift, even a small one.
  why: This is a practice Neff developed and teaches. Each line maps to one of her three parts of self-compassion, and her research suggests practicing them can reduce distress and build resilience.
