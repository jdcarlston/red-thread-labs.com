tagline: How you already feel about someone decides what you hear them say.
who:
- 'Robert L. Weiss, a couples researcher at the University of Oregon, gave this a name in 1980: sentiment override. Your overall feeling about a partner can override what they actually say or do. When the feeling is warm, a sharp comment reads as a bad day. When it has gone sour, a neutral one reads as an attack.'
- Melissa Hawkins, Sybil Carrère and John Gottman [tested it with 96 newlywed couples](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00193.x), comparing how partners read each other’s moments in a conflict conversation with how trained observers read them. Wives who felt good about their marriage read their husbands’ mildly negative moments more kindly than the observers did. Nobody’s feelings changed how they read strongly negative moments. The filter works in the gray zone, which is where most everyday talk happens.
- Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz had [156 couples watch a video of their own argument](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.20.3.494) and say, moment by moment, what they had been trying to do and what they thought their partner had been trying to do. Their guesses about each other matched what the other person said they meant only weakly to moderately. How satisfied someone was with the relationship still predicted their guesses after accounting for what the partner said they meant, and the feeling they had in that moment carried the effect.
- The filter can also make itself come true. In a [1977 study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.35.9.656), Mark Snyder, Elizabeth Decker Tanke and Ellen Berscheid had men talk by phone with women they believed, from a photo, were either attractive or not. The photos were assigned at random. Listeners who heard only the women’s side of the call rated the women whose callers thought they were attractive as warmer and friendlier. What the men expected changed how they talked, and that changed how the women answered.
- '[Michael P. Nichols](/library/michael-nichols/) names the assumptions a listener brings in as one of the main things that get in the way of hearing someone. Sentiment override is the research name for one of the strongest of them.'
concepts:
- term: Positive sentiment override
  def: A warm overall feeling that gives your partner the benefit of the doubt in unclear moments.
- term: Negative sentiment override
  def: A sour overall feeling that reads neutral or unclear moments as hostile.
- term: The gray zone
  def: Mild or unclear moments, where the filter does most of its work. Clear moments are read accurately either way.
- term: Intention and attribution
  def: What someone meant, and what you decided they meant. The two often differ.
- term: Behavioral confirmation
  def: Your expectation changes how you act, and the other person’s answer ends up confirming it.
- term: Hostile intent attribution
  def: Reading bad intent into unclear moments. Across 111 studies it is linked to aggression, though the size of the link varies.
framework:
  name: How the filter works
  intro: Sentiment override in the order it runs, from the feeling you bring to the answer you get back.
  items:
  - name: The overall feeling
    def: Built from many past moments, it sets how you expect this person to treat you.
  - name: An unclear moment
    def: A short reply, a sigh, a pause, a flat tone. Most everyday moments are unclear.
  - name: The filter fills the gap
    def: You decide what they meant, and the decision follows the feeling more than the moment.
  - name: You answer what you decided
    def: You respond to the meaning you gave it, not to what they meant.
  - name: They answer you
    def: Your response shapes theirs, and their answer can confirm the story you started with.
context: Before people can listen the way Michael Nichols describes, they need to notice the filter they listen through. In the labs we slow down unclear moments, ask what the other person was going for, and check it against what we heard. Over time that builds the warm overall feeling that makes the next unclear moment easier to read kindly. [Alison Armstrong](/library/alison-armstrong/) teaches the everyday side of this. Partnering instead of managing, and appreciating what you want more of, is how that warm feeling gets built.
exercise:
  title: Check the filter
  time: About 15 minutes, with a partner or friend
  steps:
  - Each of you picks a recent moment when the other’s words landed badly. Keep it small.
  - Write down exactly what they said, word for word. Under it, write what you heard them mean.
  - Rate how you felt about them overall that day, from 1 to 10.
  - 'Ask them: “When you said that, what were you going for?” Listen to the end, then say back what you heard until they say you’ve got it.'
  - Compare what they meant with what you heard. Name the gap out loud, without blame.
  - Switch roles. Afterward, notice whether the gap was bigger on a low day than a high one.
  why: Waldinger and Schulz found that partners’ guesses about each other’s intentions often miss, and that the miss follows how they feel about the relationship. Asking directly, and saying back what you heard, puts the other person’s own account back into the conversation.
