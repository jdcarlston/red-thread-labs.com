tagline: The stress is not the stressor. Dealing with one doesn’t deal with the other.
who:
  - Emily Nagoski holds a PhD in health behavior and trained at the Kinsey Institute. *Come As You Are* (2015) applied the [dual control model](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0149-7634(00)00024-5) of sexual response, an accelerator and a brake, to women’s everyday experience.
  - "The dual control model, from John Bancroft and Erick Janssen at the Kinsey Institute, says arousal comes from two separate systems: an accelerator that notices reasons to turn on, and brakes that notice reasons to turn off. People differ in how sensitive each one is, and those differences explain a lot of the variety in desire. Nagoski’s point for everyday life is that many people press harder on the accelerator when the real problem is the brakes."
  - With her twin sister Amelia Nagoski she wrote *Burnout* (2019), which separates the stressor from the stress. You can remove the thing that stressed you and still carry the stress, because the body’s stress response needs to complete, through movement, breath, connection, laughter, crying or creative expression.
concepts:
  - term: Completing the stress cycle
    def: The body needs to finish its stress response, not just escape the cause.
  - term: Spontaneous and responsive desire
    def: Some people feel desire out of the blue. For many, desire shows up in response to pleasure and closeness that has already started. Both are normal.
  - term: Context is everything
    def: The same touch or words can feel wonderful or awful depending on context.
  - term: The monitor
    def: An inner tracker of the gap between how things are and how you expect them to be.
framework:
  name: The dual control model
  intro: The accelerator and the brakes, from the Kinsey Institute’s research. It explains why “try harder” so rarely works, and what does.
  items:
    - name: The accelerator
      def: Notices anything that could be a reason to turn on, like what you see, hear, smell, touch, think or imagine.
    - name: The brakes
      def: Notice reasons not to, like stress, distraction, worry about your body, or risk. For many people, stress is one of the strongest brakes.
    - name: Two kinds of brakes
      def: One worries about not performing well. The other worries about what could happen, like being seen or getting hurt.
    - name: Everyone’s sensitivity differs
      def: Each person’s accelerator and brakes have their own sensitivity. A wide range is normal.
    - name: Arousal is the balance
      def: Turning on means pressing the accelerator and easing the brakes. Easing the brakes often matters more.
    - name: Context changes both
      def: Mood, trust, setting, stress and the relationship itself all change what reaches the accelerator and what hits the brakes.
context: "Nagoski gives the labs a design principle: every gathering should help people complete their stress, not just talk about it. That’s why there is movement, laughter and real connection in the room. Her accelerator-and-brake model also helps people see that “try harder” is rarely the answer. Easing the brake usually is."
exercise:
  title: Close the loop
  time: 20 to 60 minutes, once a day for a week
  steps:
    - At the end of the day, notice whether your body is still carrying stress, even if the stressful thing is over.
    - "Choose one way to complete the cycle: a brisk walk or dance, slow deep breaths, a real laugh with someone, a long hug, a good cry, or making something."
    - Do it until you feel a shift, like a sigh or a sense of being done.
    - Note which methods worked best for you.
    - By the end of the week, pick your top two and make them a habit.
  why: The Nagoskis draw on stress research to argue that the body’s stress response needs a physical or social signal that the danger has passed. Moving, breathing, laughing and connecting send that signal in ways that thinking alone often doesn’t.
